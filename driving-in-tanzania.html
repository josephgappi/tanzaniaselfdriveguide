Driving in Tanzania.

Understand the roads before you drive them. Learn Tanzania's speed limits, road signs, traffic rules, road markings and the practical realities of driving independently.

Home   /   Driving in Tanzania
Start with the basics

What is driving in Tanzania actually like?

Driving yourself around Tanzania can be one of the most rewarding ways to explore the country. It also requires more attention than simply following a navigation app.

Roads can change quickly from paved highways to gravel, rough tracks or sandy sections. You may encounter pedestrians, cyclists, motorcycles, livestock, buses, trucks and wildlife.

For international visitors, the most important things to understand are the local speed limits, road signs, road markings, driving direction and the different rules that apply inside national parks.

Field note

Do not assume that the speed you are allowed to drive on one road continues unchanged after entering a town, village, construction zone or national park.

Always look for the next sign and adjust your speed when the road environment changes.

Quick answer

Which side of the road do you drive on?

Tanzania drives on the left-hand side of the road. Visitors accustomed to right-hand traffic should take extra care when leaving junctions, entering roundabouts and joining highways.

Speed limits

Know the speed before you press the accelerator.

Tanzania's traffic rules include different speed requirements depending on where you are driving and what signs are posted. Road signs always matter, so treat the numbers below as a practical guide rather than a replacement for signs on the road.

50

km/h in built-up areas

The Road Traffic Act provides a general maximum of 50 km/h in built-up areas unless another limit is indicated.

80

km/h where applicable

An 80 km/h limit is commonly associated with applicable vehicles and road conditions outside built-up areas. Always follow the speed limit displayed on the road signs.

50

km/h maximum in national parks

TANAPA park rules state that vehicles should not exceed 50 km/h inside national parks. A lower limit may apply where posted.

Important: Inside national parks, TANAPA recommends approximately 25 km/h for game viewing. Park roads can also require much slower speeds because of wildlife, pedestrians, rough surfaces and visibility.
Essential traffic rules

Rules every self-driver should understand.

Tanzania's roads are used by many different road users. Understanding the basic rules makes driving safer and much less stressful.

01 — DRIVE LEFT

Keep left

Vehicles drive on the left-hand side of the road in Tanzania. Take particular care when turning, overtaking and entering roundabouts.

02 — SPEED

Respect the posted limit

Speed limits can change along a route. A sign showing a lower limit takes priority over a general speed expectation.

03 — OVERTAKING

Overtake only when safe

Do not overtake where visibility is poor or where road signs or markings prohibit it. Watch carefully for buses, motorcycles and oncoming traffic.

04 — PEDESTRIANS

Give way and slow down

Pedestrian crossings, schools, villages and busy settlements require particular attention. Never assume pedestrians will wait for your vehicle.

05 — ROAD MARKINGS

Watch the lines

Centre lines, edge lines, crossing markings and other road markings communicate important instructions. Do not treat painted lines as decoration.

06 — NATIONAL PARKS

Stay on designated roads

TANAPA rules require visitors to use designated roads and prohibit driving off-road. Wildlife always has priority.

Road signs matter

Keep watching the signs beside the road.

One of the most important habits for a visitor driving in Tanzania is to actively look for signs. Speed limits and other traffic instructions can be displayed along the route, and the conditions can change without much warning.

50

Speed-limit signs

A posted speed limit tells you the maximum permitted speed for that section. Reduce your speed when a lower limit is displayed.

Z

Zebra crossings

Slow down and observe pedestrian crossings. Do not pass through a crossing without respecting the instructions and pedestrians using it.

!

Warning signs

Warning signs can indicate bends, pedestrians, animals, road hazards, construction, steep sections and other conditions requiring extra care.

S

Stop signs

A stop sign requires the driver to stop and proceed only when it is safe and permitted.

↕

Road markings

Painted markings on the road can communicate restrictions, crossing areas, lane information and other instructions.

🐘

Wildlife warnings

In safari areas, warning signs may indicate wildlife or changing road conditions. Slow down and be prepared for animals near or on the road.

TSh 30,000

Example spot fine

Traffic enforcement

Ignoring road signs can result in a fine.

Basic traffic-offence information published by the Automobile Association of Tanzania, citing Tanzania Traffic Police, lists a TSh 30,000 spot fine for offences including failure to observe pedestrian crossings and failure to follow instructions conveyed by traffic signs, signals and road markings.

The same published information lists TSh 30,000 as a spot fine for speeding. Fines and enforcement procedures can change, so visitors should follow the current instructions of traffic authorities rather than relying only on an old fine table.

The safest approach is simple: slow down, read the signs, respect crossings and follow road markings.

What the road can be like

Tanzania is more than paved highways.

Your route may include several different driving environments. Allow extra time and do not plan your journey purely from straight-line distances.

Paved highways

Main highways can allow relatively efficient travel, but expect buses, trucks, motorcycles, pedestrians and changing traffic conditions.

Gravel roads

Gravel and rough roads require lower speeds, longer braking distances and greater attention to potholes, corrugations and loose surfaces.

Safari tracks

Inside parks and conservation areas, roads can be rough, dusty or muddy. Wildlife and road conditions should always determine your speed.

Villages and settlements

Slow down when approaching settlements. Pedestrians, cyclists, motorcycles, livestock and roadside activity can appear suddenly.

Rainy conditions

Heavy rain can change road conditions quickly. Mud, standing water and slippery surfaces may make a route considerably slower than expected.

Wildlife areas

In safari areas, never assume the road is clear simply because you cannot immediately see an animal. Drive slowly and remain alert.

Practical self-drive advice

Ten habits that make driving easier.

1
Watch the signs continuously. Do not rely only on your navigation application.
2
Slow down before towns and villages. Built-up areas can become busy very quickly.
3
Expect pedestrians. People may walk along the edge of the road or cross unexpectedly.
4
Watch motorcycles and bicycles. Give them sufficient space when passing.
5
Do not rush on gravel. Lower speed gives you more control.
6
Be careful when overtaking. Make sure you can see far enough ahead.
7
Watch for livestock. Cattle, goats and other animals can enter the road.
8
Do not drive off designated park roads. TANAPA park rules prohibit off-road driving.
9
Do not assume the GPS knows everything. Check the road itself and allow for changing conditions.
10
Leave enough time. A route that looks short on a map can take much longer on rough or busy roads.
Sources & verification

Information should be checked, not guessed.

This guide is intended as practical information for visitors planning to drive in Tanzania. Traffic rules, penalties, park regulations and road conditions can change. Where possible, information should be checked against official Tanzanian authorities and current park regulations before travelling.

Key references used for this page include the Tanzania Road Traffic Act, Tanzania National Parks Authority (TANAPA) park rules and Tanzania road-safety information published by Tanzanian authorities and road-safety organisations.
